Good question — the answer comes from how link budgets work.

The Friis equation treats TX and RX antenna gains symmetrically. Every dB of antenna efficiency is additive regardless of which end it sits on:

```
received power = TX power + TX antenna gain + path gain + RX antenna gain
```

So if the dual band antenna costs roughly 15 dB at 867 MHz (which is what the math suggests from your 30 dB two-antenna gap):

| TX antenna | RX antenna | RSSI vs best case |
|---|---|---|
| Rubber ducky | Rubber ducky | **baseline** (your −71 dBm) |
| Rubber ducky | Dual band | −15 dB → roughly −86 dBm |
| Dual band | Rubber ducky | −15 dB → roughly −86 dBm |
| Dual band | Dual band | −30 dB → your −102 dBm |

Mixing one of each gives you half the penalty — still 15 dB down from your best result, but not as bad as both being wrong.

The reason the two cases (dual band TX vs dual band RX) give identical results is **antenna reciprocity** — a passive antenna that radiates poorly at a given frequency also receives poorly at that same frequency, by the same amount. There is no way to "compensate" a weak TX antenna by putting a better antenna on the RX side more than you could do the reverse.

**Practical takeaway**: for your competition flight, run rubber ducky on both the CanSat and the ground station. Upgrading only one end still leaves you 15 dB short of where you could be.

## Short answer

You **cannot** rescue the dual band antenna to rubber-ducky level. The physics caps how much a matching network can recover, and 30 dB is far above that cap. ## Why 30 dB cannot be tuned away

Mismatch loss saturates. This is the whole argument:

| VSWR | Reflection coeff Γ | Mismatch loss |
|---|---|---|
| 2:1 | 0.333 | 0.51 dB |
| 3:1 | 0.500 | 1.25 dB |
| 5:1 | 0.667 | 2.55 dB |
| 10:1 | 0.818 | **4.81 dB** |
| 20:1 | 0.905 | 7.4 dB |
| 50:1 | 0.961 | 11.1 dB |
| 100:1 | 0.980 | 14.0 dB |

`ML = −10 log₁₀(1 − Γ²)`, `Γ = (VSWR−1)/(VSWR+1)`

Your gap is 30 dB round trip, so ~15 dB per antenna. Reaching 15 dB from mismatch alone needs VSWR above 100:1 — essentially an open circuit. Real detuned whips sit at 5:1 to 15:1, which is 2.5 to 6 dB. **So most of your 30 dB is not reflection. It is radiation inefficiency.**

That distinction is the whole answer. A matching network fixes reflection. It cannot fix efficiency. If that antenna is physically a 2.4 GHz element (~31 mm radiator), at 867 MHz it is 0.09 λ — electrically short, radiation resistance around 1–2 Ω. Conjugate-matching it means forcing current through a loading coil whose own ohmic resistance is comparable to or larger than the radiation resistance, so most of your power heats the coil. The literature on electrically short monopoles is unanimous on this: matching networks adjust impedance but cannot recover losses inherent to low radiation resistance, and the network's own loss cannot be eliminated, only minimised.

Realistic best case with a hand-tuned L-network: you recover maybe **4 to 7 dB of the 30**, at the cost of a narrowband, hand-built, temperature-sensitive circuit in a vehicle that gets shaken and dropped. That is a bad trade for a flight article.

## What the rules actually constrain

This matters and I want to be precise, because it changes what "optimisation" is even allowed.

Your assignment locks frequency, spreading factor, bandwidth, coding rate, sync word and power. It says nothing about antennas — **antenna choice is a free design variable.** But there is an indirect ceiling: in the EU 868 g1 sub-band the limit is +14 dBm **ERP**, which is +16.15 dBm EIRP. ERP is referenced to a dipole, EIRP to an isotropic radiator, and the 2.15 dB between them is exactly a dipole's gain.

So if your assigned +14 dBm is *radiated* rather than *conducted at the PA*, then:

- An antenna at **≤ 2.15 dBi** keeps you compliant with no derating.
- A **5 dBi** gain antenna puts you ~2.9 dB over the ceiling.

A plain quarter-wave or half-wave rubber ducky is 2.15 dBi or less. It is the compliance-safe choice *and* the best performer. That is a rare case where the correct engineering answer and the correct legal answer coincide, so take it.

**Do not chase a high-gain antenna to buy link margin.** That is the one antenna change that could genuinely put you out of compliance.

## What you can legitimately optimise, ranked by dB

Everything here is mechanical. **There is no firmware change involved at all** — every radio register stays exactly where the organisers put it.

**1. Check for an RP-SMA / SMA mismatch first — potentially 20+ dB, costs nothing**

Before any theory: RP-SMA and SMA look identical but the centre conductor is reversed. Mate an RP-SMA antenna to an SMA jack and the centre pin never makes contact — the signal couples only capacitively across an air gap. That produces exactly the kind of catastrophic, otherwise-inexplicable loss you measured. Look into both connectors. If one has a centre *pin* and the other also has a *pin* (or both are sockets), that is your 30 dB, and the dual band antenna may be fine after all.

**2. Ground plane on the ground station — 3 to 6 dB**

A quarter-wave whip is half an antenna. It needs a counterpoise or the coax braid radiates instead, distorting the pattern and dumping power into the ground. A published 868 MHz build uses an 82 mm radiator with four 92 mm radials — radials about 12% longer than the element, drooped rather than flat, which pulls feedpoint impedance toward 50 Ω. One builder reported other nodes coming up **6 to 9 dB in SNR** after fitting one.

**3. Tilt the ground antenna 30–45° off vertical — recovers the apogee null**

Already in README §4. A vertical whip has a deep null along its own axis. With both whips vertical you get a null at both ends at once, which is the classic apogee dropout. This is worth more than any dB figure suggests because it converts a total outage into a working link.

**4. Get the ground antenna high and clear — several dB**

Fresnel zone clearance. Ground reflections at 867 MHz are strong.

**5. Keep the CanSat whip away from the Ø110 mm plates — unknown, possibly large**

This one is specific to your build. Those plates are almost exactly one wavelength in circumference at 867 MHz. If they are aluminium, they are resonant parasitic loops sitting right next to your radiator, and they will reshape the pattern in ways you have not characterised. Worth a bench check with the flight stack assembled versus the antenna held clear.

1. **Power: `POWER PAD` (+10 dBm).** Not flight power. You're not in flight, and your standing rule is strict compliance. This is also the conservative choice — flight gets +14 dBm, so whatever you measure improves by **4 dB** on the day.
2. **Ground station:** highest point you can reach. Antenna tilted **30–45° off vertical**, not straight up — a vertical whip has a deep null along its own axis, and with both whips vertical you get a null at both ends at once.
3. **CanSat end:** on a pole at 1.5–2 m, held clear of the aluminium plates and away from your body. Not on the ground, not in a bag.
4. **Line of sight.** At 867 MHz one building is 20–30 dB. Your first test through a closed wall cost you exactly that.
5. **Steps:** 100 m → 250 m → 500 m → 1 km → 1.5 km → 2 km. Dwell **60 seconds** at each (≈45–60 packets), stationary.
6. **Two people, phones.** Log the serial output to a file — the RX sketch already prints the CSV you need.

### Judge it on PER, not RSSI

`HANDOFF.md` says this and it's the right call. RSSI tells you almost nothing about whether the link works; LoRa holds together until it suddenly doesn't. Your pass criterion:

- **≥ 95% packets received at 1.5 km** — link is good
- **≥ 90% at 2 km** — you've met the requirement with ground-test pessimism on top

SNR is your early warning. It stays positive with margin, then falls off a cliff. When SNR margin drops below ~5 dB you're near the edge.

## Link margin, definition


## B. Predicted link budget — the one CDR wants

### Step 1 — derive your sensitivity, don't just quote it

A jury will ask where −132 dBm came from. Derive it:

```
S = −174 + 10·log₁₀(BW) + NF + SNR_min
  = −174 + 10·log₁₀(125000) + 5.5 + (−15)
  = −174 + 50.97 + 5.5 − 15
  = −132.5 dBm
```

- −174 dBm/Hz — thermal noise floor at 290 K
- 50.97 dB — your mandated 125 kHz bandwidth
- 5.5 dB — LR1121 noise figure with boosted RX gain (approximate; the only soft term)
- −15 dB — SF10 demodulator threshold

This reconciles with the datasheet SF10/BW125 figure to within 0.5 dB. **Use −132 dBm** and show this derivation.

### Step 2 — pick the right path loss model

**Free space** (in flight, both ends clear of ground):
```
FSPL = 32.45 + 20·log₁₀(d_km) + 20·log₁₀(f_MHz)
```

**Two-ray ground reflection** (ground testing, and after landing):
```
PL = 40·log₁₀(d_m) − 20·log₁₀(h_t) − 20·log₁₀(h_r)
```
Valid beyond `d_b = 4π·h_t·h_r/λ`, λ = 0.3458 m. Below `d_b`, use FSPL. **Always take whichever is larger.**

Using the wrong model is the single most common error in a CanSat link budget — it's the difference between 20 and 40 dB/decade.

### Step 3 — the budget chain

| Term | Sign | Your value |
|---|---|---|
| TX power | + | +14 dBm flight / +10 dBm pad |
| CanSat antenna gain | + | +3.58 dBi (dual band) or ~2 dBi (ducky) |
| CanSat feed loss | − | 0.6 dB (u.FL→RP-SMA pigtail) |
| Path loss | − | from Step 2 |
| Ground antenna gain | + | +11 dBi (Yagi) or ~2 dBi (whip) |
| Ground feed loss | − | 2.1 dB (3 m RG58 @ 867 MHz) |
| **= Received power** | | dBm |

| Impairment | Allowance | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Polarisation | 3 dB | tumbling whip vs linear Yagi |
| Blockage | 3 dB | body, structure, terrain |
| Fading | 6 dB | multipath |
| **Total** | **12 dB** | |

### Step 5 — margin

```
Gross margin = P_rx − S
Net margin   = Gross − 12 dB
```

**Net margin > 10 dB = the link closes.**

## What's different

**The packet is now the real one.** The old sketch sent a filler string. Airtime — and therefore error rate at a given signal level — depends on payload length, so a wrong-sized test packet measures a link you aren't flying. `buildPacket()` now emits the exact layout from `Config.h` §6:

Twenty fields, base-36, `\r\n` terminated, padded in the optional-data region to the 99-byte flight wall. Values are synthetic; **the length and field structure are real**, and those are what decide airtime.
