The antenna was simulated as it is actually built — the RadioMaster dual-band dipole on the vehicle centre axis, hung from the top electronics plate pointing down, inside the fitted ABS shell and the aluminium plate stack. This is the pattern of the antenna bolted to this CanSat, not a datasheet figure in free space. Against the vendor's 3.58 dBi free-space number, the airframe costs about 1 dB and reshapes the pattern — it does not absorb, as the 99.7 % efficiency shows.

3. The nadir null and the link budget

Because the dipole points down, its deepest null points straight at a ground station directly below. Quantified from the solved pattern, the null is only about 2° wide, and the link closes through it. At 1 km slant range, +14 dBm flight power, dual-band whips both ends (Yagi column shown as the upgrade):

Even in the worst case — the vehicle directly overhead, its axial null aimed at the ground station — the link holds with positive margin, and recovers to tens of dB within a couple of degrees of motion. A momentary RSSI dip on a near-overhead pass is this null, not a fault.

4. Antenna-to-antenna isolation

Coupling from the 867 MHz comm antenna into the GNSS patch is −49.3 dB, past the −45 dB threshold below which a LoRa burst would desense the GNSS receiver. The two antennas can run simultaneously — no firmware rule to blank GNSS during transmit is required.

5. The ABS shell is modelled correctly

The perforated shell was solved two independent ways — the real 4787-face hex-lattice print, and a smooth tube carrying its effective permittivity (εr 1.83). They agree to 0.04 dB in peak gain and within ±0.02 dB across the whole pattern, proving the fast model is faithful rather than assumed.

One honest caveat for the panel. The model contains the bare 100 mm radiating element with no matching network, so its raw input match (S11) is not the flight antenna's — the real RadioMaster part is internally matched to VSWR 1.25 by means the vendor does not publish. Gain, efficiency, pattern shape and isolation are trustworthy; absolute S11/VSWR from the model are not, and are not quoted as flight values.
